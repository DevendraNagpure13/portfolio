WORK 04

An Improved Phenomenological Description of Electroplasticity Accounting Internal Stress Evolution

Aluminum alloys like AA5083 are great for cars and boats — light, strong, corrosion-resistant — but they can be stubborn to form, resisting deformation and limiting how far you can push them before they crack. Turns out a quick pulse of electric current can help: it drops the metal's flow stress on the spot, making it noticeably easier to deform and improving how much it can stretch before failing. This effect has been studied for years, but nobody had cleanly separated why it works — is it just resistive heating, or is the current actually reorganizing the metal's internal defect structure? In this paper, we built a test that isolates that second, non-thermal effect and tracked how it builds up as we varied the current and pulse length. That let us develop an improved physics-based model — one where the internal state of the material actually evolves realistically pulse after pulse, instead of assuming it stays fixed — and we backed it up with fracture surface imaging and microscopy showing exactly why the material also becomes noticeably more ductile. The upshot: a clearer, more predictive picture of how electric current can make aluminum forming easier, more efficient, and less prone to cracking.

WORK 03

A Novel Methodology to Estimate the Athermal Contribution During Electric-Assisted Deformation of AA 1050 Alloy.

In this research, electroplastic test with and without stress relaxation is utilized to decouple the rate-dependent component of stress drop. The stress relaxation exhausts the effect of rate-dependent component by dislocation overcoming the short-range obstacle. The net contribution due to electric current after stress relaxation is rate-independent or athermal. No additional assumptions are included to derive the athermal component. The methodology is successfully demonstrated through electric-assisted deformation tests using AA1050 alloy samples.

WORK 02

FEA of an L-shaped bracket

Developed a finite element analysis solver from scratch in Python to simulate stress and deformation in a 2D L-bracket plate under combined loading. Implemented the full FEM pipeline — Constant Strain Triangle (CST) element formulation, global stiffness matrix assembly, plane-stress constitutive modeling for two distinct materials, and penalty-method displacement boundary conditions — without relying on external FEM libraries. Solved a 22-node, 28-element mesh under a mixed load case (two 50 kN point loads and a 500 N lateral load), then post-processed nodal displacements into element-wise strain, stress, and von Mises stress fields to identify a stress concentration of ~650 MPa at a material interface near a geometric notch. Built a custom Python visualization pipeline to render mesh, deformed shape, and stress contour plots, translating raw linear algebra output into an interpretable engineering result.

WORK 01

Simulation and Modeling of WAAM

This project developed and validated a coupled thermal–structural finite element model to predict residual stress and distortion in Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing, comparing Single-Wire (S-WAAM) and Twin-Wire (T-WAAM) configurations, using ANSYS with APDL scripting to implement Goldak's double-ellipsoidal moving heat source alongside a matched Gaussian pressure load that captures arc force effects on the weld pool, feeding thermal history from the moving heat source as load input into a sequentially coupled transient thermal → structural FEA pipeline to predict stress and deformation; the analysis showed that peak transverse residual stress consistently forms a characteristic M-shaped profile near the weld centerline that matches published experimental trends, that T-WAAM produces higher heat input, residual stress, and distortion than S-WAAM due to overlapping heat sources from the two electrodes, and that maximum distortion occurs at the end of each deposition pass and worsens with unidirectional, repeated passes — informing a recommendation to alternate deposition direction with cooling intervals — resulting in a validated simulation framework that helps predict and mitigate residual stress and distortion in WAAM builds and informs clamping strategy, deposition sequencing, and process parameter selection for large-scale metal 3D printing.
